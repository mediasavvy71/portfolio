Remitra™: Influencing Product Strategy Through Facilitation and Systems Thinking

Remitra™ set out to move healthcare purchasing, invoicing and payment from paper and phone calls to a connected digital system. An external UX consultant owned the overall UX strategy. As Principal UX Designer, my job was to make that strategy stronger.

I facilitated the conversations between the consultant and Premier's business, product and engineering teams, questioned assumptions, contributed my own recommendations and guided a junior designer. As the consultant's designs grew more complex, I pushed for us to validate them with real users before going further, and proposed a usability research plan. Along the way I identified a deeper problem: the organization defined products by feature lists before it understood how people actually worked.

1The Context

A healthcare financial system held together by paper and handoffs

Hospitals buy from thousands of suppliers. Every purchase moves through a chain of handoffs between two companies: a purchase order, an acknowledgement, a shipment, an invoice and a payment. When we started, much of that chain still ran on fax, email, mail and manual data entry.

Remitra™ was Premier's initiative to connect those fragmented steps. The opportunity was clear: better workflow integration, financial visibility, automation and interoperability between health systems and suppliers. Each break in the chain meant mismatched invoices, delayed payments and rework on both sides.

Two Companies, One Transaction

Every transaction is shared between a health system and a supplier, each with its own systems, roles and incentives.

Many Ways to Buy

Orders arrived electronically, by fax or email, or were entered by hand. Each path had different rules and different gaps.

Visibility Lost Between Steps

Once a document left one system, its status was hard to trace. Suppliers chased payments and buyers chased corrections.

2The Challenge

The product was complex. The way we defined products made it harder.

The product challenge was real: many user types, many purchasing paths and strict financial rules. But as the work progressed, I saw a second problem underneath it, in how the organization decided what to build.

Business lines tended to define products as lists of features and technical requirements. Far less effort went into understanding how users actually solved their problems day to day.

Feature-Driven Development

Teams tried to accommodate every scenario and every user type at once. The result was feature-rich experiences that could lose focus and become hard to use.

Long Product Lifecycles

Enterprise products can take years to design, build and run through their contracts. Leaders often moved on before anyone could judge whether their original decisions worked.

Limited User Understanding

Business stakeholders didn't have established ways to investigate users' needs and workflows, so assumptions could become requirements without being validated.

The scope the product had to carry
Trying to solve every problem for every user produces a product that solves none of them well.
My critique of the feature-first approach
3Understanding the System

Mapping three ways to buy, to find what we didn't know

With the consulting designer and cross-functional stakeholders, we mapped the procurement models Remitra™ had to support, step by step, from purchase order to payment. The diagram was a shared artifact. My part was to contribute recommendations, question assumptions and help keep the discussion moving toward the gaps.

4Challenging the Approach · Enabling Better Discovery

Making it easier for business teams to understand users before defining solutions

I advocated for understanding user behavior before deciding what a product should contain. Telling teams to "do more research" doesn't change much on its own, though. Most business stakeholders had no practical starting point.

So I recommended borrowing something they already recognized. UX deliverables like personas, journey maps and usability reports are familiar across the industry. I proposed structured research templates, based on those deliverables, that business teams could fill in themselves.

The templates were never meant to replace UX researchers, and a completed template isn't validated research. The goal was to make user-centered practice approachable, and to give business teams a real share of the responsibility for understanding the people using their products.

Proposed Research Template · Prompts
01
User needs and behaviors. Who uses this, and what do they actually do today?
02
Existing workflows. What steps, tools and handoffs are involved now?
03

5Strategic Collaboration

An internal thought partner to a consultant-led strategy

The external consultant developed the overarching UX strategy: a more connected financial experience built on workflow integration, interoperability, automation, greater financial visibility and less fragmentation between transactions.

My role was to make that strategy hold up inside Premier. I brought internal context the consultant couldn't see from outside, challenged ideas that didn't fit how teams or users worked, and contributed recommendations of my own. I wasn't there to approve the work. I was there to improve it.

6Validating Early

Testing the direction before we built any further on it

As I watched the consultant's work grow more complex, I became concerned that we were stacking design decisions on top of assumptions no supplier had confirmed. The longer we waited, the more expensive it would be to change course.

So I suggested we start validating the designs before going much further. I proposed a research plan for moderated usability testing, and had the junior designer build a clickable prototype of the supplier workflows to put in front of real users.

The plan's purpose was specific: validate the Remitra workflows as they appeared in the prototype, with the people who actually do invoice management, so the results could inform Premier's invoice management strategy.

What the tests were designed to validate

Purchase order tests

Can suppliers successfully:

  • Recognize they have received a new purchase order
  • Accept a purchase order with changes
  • Send a PO acknowledgement to the customer

And what roadblocks do they hit along the way?

Invoice tests

Can suppliers successfully:

  • Create an invoice from the PO acknowledgement
  • Send the invoice to the customer
  • Confirm that the customer has paid

And what roadblocks do they hit along the way?

How the sessions were planned
Moderated, remoteOne participant at a time, with observers kept to a minimum and silent.
60-minute sessionsA short briefing, the tasks, then time for questions at the end.
Think-aloudParticipants vocalize their thoughts and read on-screen content aloud. No help from the moderator.
Mixed-fidelity prototypeA clickable demo of the real workflows, not a working application.

Participants: people responsible for fulfilling purchase orders at their organization, and those responsible for reconciling accounts receivable.

Reflection

Principal-level work isn't measured in the strategies I author. It's measured in the questions I make the team answer.

On Remitra, the most useful thing I did was often to slow a conversation down long enough to ask what we actually knew about the people we were designing for. The strategy belonged to someone else. The quality of the thinking around it was something I could raise.

What I'd do next

  • Pilot the research templates on a single business line, so their effect could be measured rather than argued.
