How I lead design.

My role is to turn complex product challenges into focused, actionable work. I connect research with product strategy, guide teams through key decisions, and stay hands-on from early concepts through implementation.

How I work

The Process: Structured to Scale with Ambiguity

I use seven connected activities as a toolkit, not a fixed sequence. The depth of each activity depends on what the team already knows, the impact of getting a decision wrong, and the evidence needed to proceed. Some work calls for deeper discovery; other work can move directly into prototyping or delivery.

Keep delivery moving while reducing uncertainty.

I separate reversible, low-risk choices from assumptions that could significantly affect users or the business. Teams can act on reliable information today while we investigate consequential unknowns through targeted research, prototypes, and feedback as the work unfolds.

01

Frame

Align on the user problem, business outcomes, assumptions, constraints, and success metrics.

02

Discover

Interview users and stakeholders; review analytics and existing research; map people, processes, data, systems, and decisions.

03

Synthesize

Turn findings into journeys, workflows, opportunity areas, and a shared experience strategy.

04

Explore

Compare concepts, information architecture, and task flows against user needs, feasibility, accessibility, and business goals.

05

Validate

Prototype at the lowest useful fidelity. Test realistic tasks and risky assumptions; use feedback to iterate.

06

Deliver & Launch

Finalize UI, content, states, accessibility checks, and engineering specs. Stay engaged through build, QA, and release.

07

Measure & Iterate

Review task success, time on task, errors, adoption, and business KPIs. Feed learnings into the next cycle.

Principles: Start with the problem, not a solution. Test behavior, not just preferences. Use established design-system patterns and accessibility checks. Collaborate with engineering before and after handoff.

Evidence from the work

Making thoughtful tradeoffs when conditions aren’t ideal.

Real projects rarely offer ideal conditions. These examples show how I weighed competing needs, limited time, technical feasibility, and imperfect evidence—making deliberate choices about what to prioritize, what to simplify, and what to revisit.

Strategic alignment · Healthcare quality

Balancing competing priorities

Product leadership needed executive visibility, quality teams needed granular measures, and hospital users needed to spot failing measures quickly. Rather than optimize for one audience at the expense of the others, I interviewed stakeholders, mapped workflows and CMS reporting, and used shared use cases and journey maps to define a direction that balanced those needs. The tradeoff was prioritizing common, actionable workflows over trying to satisfy every request at once.

Delivery tradeoffs · Healthcare analytics

Trading interaction depth for timely delivery

We had designed a highly interactive performance-data experience, but engineering estimates showed it could not ship on schedule. I partnered with product and engineering to protect the core workflow while simplifying the implementation into Power BI. We gave up some interaction richness in the near term to deliver useful functionality on time, while keeping the richer experience on the roadmap.

Research judgment · Limited access

Moving forward without ideal research access

Direct access to users is not always available at the start of a project. Waiting for perfect evidence can stall progress; treating assumptions as facts creates another risk. I use support insights, business analysts, analytics, competitive products, and subject-matter experts to form provisional hypotheses, make uncertainty explicit, and design a path to validate with users when access becomes possible.

Decision facilitation · Concept exploration

Choosing among imperfect design options

Stakeholders can favor different solutions, and no concept maximizes usability, implementation speed, and business value at once. I develop alternative concepts—including rapid AI-assisted prototypes where useful—and compare them with stakeholders and users. Making the tradeoffs visible helps teams choose a defensible direction instead of defaulting to the loudest opinion or the first idea.
