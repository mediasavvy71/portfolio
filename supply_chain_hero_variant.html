From database report to supply chain decision tool

Approving a requisition is a simple decision: what is being requested, what it costs, and whether it should go ahead. In Premier's legacy ERP that decision sat behind three menu levels, a 22-column report and a dialog with two separate checkboxes. I evaluated the full approval flow screen by screen, then rebuilt it around how approvers actually decide.

At a glance

The problem

Approving a routine requisition in Premier's legacy ERP meant three menu levels, a 22-column queue, nine tabs and an approve/reject dialog where both boxes could be checked.

My role & team

Principal UX Designer. Owned the Buyer experience: buyer interviews, the heuristic evaluation, journey mapping and redesigned screens on the PINC AI Design System.

Key decision

Rebuild the flow around the five questions an approver asks, and replace the two-checkbox dialog with clearly named Approve and Reject actions on the review page.

Outcome

40% fewer steps across primary procurement tasks, a 23% productivity improvement, and an approval queue cut from 22 to 10 columns.

1The Task

One everyday decision, buried under the structure of the system

The ERP did a lot: requisitions, procurement, approval routing, dollar limits, approver groups. The problem was not missing features. To finish a routine approval, users had to know how the ERP organised its modules, find their way through layers of menus, read dense tables, and piece together the facts they needed to decide on their own. The software made users work the way the system was built.

"Every morning I dig through three menus just to find my queue. Then I'm scanning twenty-some columns to work out what's urgent, and clicking through tabs to see if it's even on contract. Just show me what needs my attention."
Maria Delgado · Buyer persona · Mid-size Regional Hospital

Evaluation scope

I picked the requisition approval path because it is short, high-stakes and easy to test. It involves one role and one decision, and success is clear: the decision is correct, recorded and confirmed. I reviewed the six screens an approver passes through, from logging in to submitting a decision.

A redesign that throws out working parts loses users' trust. These strengths carried into the new design:

2Heuristic Evaluation

Six screens, rated by how much each one gets in the way of the decision

I walked through each screen as an approver and recorded where it made the task harder, how severe that was, and what should change. Click any screen to enlarge it.

01 · ERP dashboard

High

Finding: Shows EDI exceptions, invoice types and match exceptions as month-by-month reports, with no indication of what needs the user's attention. Large empty panels sit next to cramped navigation and tiny labels.

Recommendation: Role-based KPIs, summaries of exceptions the user can act on, and a prominent "Pending my approval" entry point.

02 · Main navigation

Medium

Finding: Labels such as "Work in Materials" and "Work in Accounts Payable" follow the system's internal structure. Users have to know which module holds each function.

Recommendation: Replace them with task-based labels such as Requisitions, Purchase Orders and Approvals.

03 · Requisitioning submenu

High

Finding: At least 14 links mix daily tasks ("My Requisition Approval Queue") with admin settings ("Approval Dollar Limits", "Mass Approver Change"), all given the same visual weight.

Recommendation: Group links by what the user wants to do (Create, Track, Approve, Configure) and put the most-used actions first.

04 · Approval queue

Critical

Finding: 22 data columns, including two "Out of Office" columns, Approver Buddy and Commodity Code, hide the main question: which request do I review next? The "Review Requisition" link is small and repeated on every row, and the rows are sorted by requisition number rather than urgency.

Recommendation: Put requester, description, amount, need-by date and status first. Add useful filters and make each row a clear action.

05 · Requisition details

High

Finding: Nine tabs, about two dozen form fields and a 20-column line-item table. The total cost, labelled "Total Estimated Extended Cost", gets the same weight as Pick Cycle and Fund Code.

Recommendation: Start with a decision summary at the top, then line items, financial details and supporting documents.

06 · Approve / reject dialog

Critical

Finding: A modal titled "Do You Want To Approve Or Reject Requisition At This Time?" offers two independent checkboxes, "Approve Now" and "Reject Now", plus a generic Submit button. Nothing on screen prevents both being checked, and the button never says which outcome it will record.

Recommendation: Make approve and reject mutually exclusive choices with buttons that name the action, and show clear success or error feedback.

4Before & After

Each stage of the journey, redesigned to answer one question

These redesigned screens are part of the Buyer experience I owned and were built with the PINC AI Design System. Each stage below puts the legacy screen next to the new one and lists the evaluation findings the change addresses. Click any screen to enlarge it.

Stage 01

From reports to a to-do list

The legacy dashboard showed six months of EDI and invoice statistics. The new one opens on what needs action today: counts, alerts and shortcuts to start work.

What needs my attention?
Before
  • Four 2015 report tables showing historic statistics
  • No approval count or entry point
  • Release notes take up a third of the screen
After
  • KPI row leads with Approvals: 11, plus receiving and exceptions
  • Dated to-do list, with an Actions menu on each alert
  • Create Requisition / PO / Receive Orders kept to the side
AddressesDashboard doesn't highlight next actionsLarge empty areasVisibility of system status
Stage 02

KPIs that work as navigation

In the legacy ERP, the only way to find out whether anything needed attention was to open each module and look. In the redesign, the KPI row answers that question as soon as the dashboard loads, and each count is a link straight to the filtered list of items behind it.

Is there anything I need to deal with?
Before · Check every module
  • No counts anywhere. Users opened Requisitioning, Receiving, Invoicing and Purchasing one at a time to check for work
  • Each check meant drilling into a submenu, then scanning a report, often to find nothing
  • Urgent items stayed hidden until someone went looking for them
After · Counts that link to the work
Approvals 11→Approvals worklist, filtered to Pending
Receiving 24→Orders awaiting receipt
Invoice Exceptions 14→Invoices needing resolution
PO Exceptions 12→Purchase orders with issues
On Contract Spend $77K→Contract spend analytics
  • A non-zero count shows there is work before the user clicks anything
  • One click goes from the count to the exact items that need action, with no menu in between
  • A zero means no trip is needed, which the legacy menus could never tell you

Stage 03

From modules to tasks

The approval queue used to be four levels down a module tree. Now it is one of six task names in the top bar.

Stage 04

From database report to worklist

The queue went from 22 columns to 10. Each row now shows what the request is, how much it costs, who asked and whether it has been escalated, with a filter under every column.

What am I approving?
Before
  • 22 columns, including Approver Buddy, Commodity Code and two "Out of Office" columns
  • Descriptions repeat "Created from Shopping Cart ERP_NA Import"
  • Sorted by requisition number; small "Review Requisition" link on every row
After
  • 10 columns, with Total, Description and Requester easy to see
  • Pending / approval-type filter at the top, inline filters on each column
  • Attachment icon and row menu for quick context

Stage 05

From form to decision summary

Nine tabs became one page. The facts needed to approve sit at the top, followed by the line items, cut from 20 columns to the five that matter for the decision.

Is it appropriate?
Before
  • Nine tabs and about two dozen fields with equal weight
  • Total hidden in "Total Estimated Extended Cost"
  • Line-item table needs horizontal scrolling across 20 columns
After
  • Summary first: number, status, Total Cost, Requested By
  • Plain-language description, organization and department
  • Line items: item, cost, quantity, unit and expense code only
AddressesInformation spread across screensTechnical labelsSmall text & controls
Stage 06

From checkboxes to explicit actions

The riskiest pattern in the flow was removed. The decision is now made on the review page itself, with buttons that name the outcome. Two checkboxes and a generic Submit are gone.

What action should I take?
Before
  • Separate modal that repeats data already on the page
  • "Approve Now" and "Reject Now" can both be checked
  • "Submit" doesn't say which outcome it will record
After
  • Approve (primary) and Reject (secondary) are separate, clearly named actions
  • Decision made in context, with the totals and line items still in view
  • Cancel and Save kept apart from the decision
AddressesApprove/reject not mutually exclusiveError preventionUser control & freedom
5Validation & Outcomes

Measured where we could, with a plan to test the rest

40% fewer task steps

Primary procurement workflows went from deep, many-click flows to short paths suited to each role, which addressed the most common complaint from research.

23% productivity improvement

Showing work up front and cutting navigation gave buyers and approvers back time each day.

One visual language with PINC AI

Shared components meant the approvals worklist, review page and dashboard looked and behaved the same, which fixed the inconsistency flagged in the evaluation.

Stronger demos

Sales and demo teams reported a positive reaction from prospective clients, which supported Premier's goal of winning back market share.

Usability study design

A focused qualitative study of 12–15 participants, with some overlap between roles, to confirm the severity ratings and set a baseline before the next iteration.

Core tasks

  1. Find a requisition waiting for your approval.
  2. Find a requisition by its number.
  3. Identify the requester, items, vendor and total.
  4. Approve a requisition after reviewing it.
  5. Reject a requisition and give a reason.
  6. Confirm your decision was recorded.

Proposed success targets

Task completion95%All six core tasks
First-attempt success90%No backtracking
Critical decision errors0Wrong approve / reject
User confidence4 / 5Post-task rating

These targets are illustrative and should be set against a baseline from the legacy workflow. Time on task, navigation errors, assistance needed and post-task ease ratings are also recorded.

Reflection

The ERP wasn't missing features. It made people work the way the system was built.

Turning a heuristic review into a ranked list of issues kept the redesign scoped and testable, and keeping measured results apart from inferred ones kept the story honest.

What I'd do next

  • Run the 12–15 participant usability study with approvers, procurement specialists, requesters and administrators to confirm the severity ratings and set a baseline.
  • Measure the redesign against the proposed targets: 95% task completion, 90% first-attempt success and zero wrong approve/reject decisions.
