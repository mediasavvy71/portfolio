UX Design Architecture · Proposal to leadership

Introducing Design Operations

A practical 12-month path from strong individual product areas to one connected platform experience.

[Bill Teppig] · Principal UX Designer

[03/28/24]

Open by naming the goal, not the discipline: one coherent customer experience across every product area. The dots: twenty designers on a grid. Today each dot is strong on its own; the job is the connective tissue between them.

Business outcomes

What the business gets from DesignOps

Faster time to market

Reused patterns and research cut redundant design and rework, so teams ship sooner.

Lower delivery cost

Less duplicated research, fewer one-off components, less design-to-dev rework.

Stronger retention

A coherent experience across products lowers friction for customers who use more than one.

Lower support load

Consistent, validated workflows mean fewer confused users and fewer tickets.

Reduced risk

Accessibility and quality built into the process, not caught after release.

Visible UX ROI

Leadership sees design impact in business terms and knows where to invest next.

Introducing DesignOps · Why

02

Lead with outcomes the business already cares about: speed, cost, retention, support load, risk and visibility. Each one ties back to a later part of the plan: shared patterns and research reuse drive speed and cost; a coherent cross-product experience drives retention and lower support load; the Definition of UX Quality reduces risk; the quarterly UX Impact Review makes ROI visible. Baseline these during the audit so later gains can be shown with real numbers.

The challenge

The risk isn't twenty designers working differently. It's twenty designers optimizing their own areas with no mechanism for one coherent customer experience.

The organizational risk is not inconsistency between people. It is that each product area is optimized locally while customers buy and experience a connected platform.

Positioning

Introducing DesignOps · Why

04

Don't open by announcing "DesignOps." Designers often hear "operations" and picture tickets, templates, meetings and approvals. Frame it as removing friction so designers spend more time designing, and the payoff is a more connected cross-product experience.

What DesignOps is

People, process and craft, at scale

NN/g defines DesignOps as orchestrating people, processes and craft to amplify design's value and impact at scale, and recommends starting with internal research rather than imposed process.

Introducing DesignOps · Roadmap

06

Phases overlap deliberately. Diagnose and charter in the first six weeks; spend the bulk of the effort building the operating system; then layer in governance, impact reporting and, only once value is proven, dedicated roles.

Phase 1 · Weeks 1–4 · Diagnose

Run a UX operating-model audit

How work flows

  • How does work reach you?
  • Who determines priorities?
  • How do you collaborate with PM and Engineering?
  • Who decides something is ready for engineering?

How knowledge flows

  • When does research happen?
  • Where do research findings live?
  • Where do designs live?
  • How do you know another team hasn't already solved this?

How quality is judged

  • How are designs reviewed?
  • How is accessibility validated?
  • How do you use the design system?
  • What takes time but adds little UX value?
  • How do you know whether what you shipped worked?

Also interview Product, Engineering, Customer Success, subject-matter experts and UX leadership. Look for systemic friction, not complaints about individuals.

Introducing DesignOps · Phase 1

07

Interview every designer, or at minimum a representative from every product area, and ask everyone the same questions so answers are comparable. This follows NN/g's DesignOps planning pattern: survey, interviews, map the design process, identify waste and value, prioritize opportunities. Source: NN/g DesignOps Planning Workbook.

Turn the qualitative audit into a measurable baseline across eight dimensions. Avoid a maturity score that ranks teams; a three-level rating keeps the conversation on the system. Fill the Today column from audit results.

Phase 2 · Weeks 4–6 · Prioritize

Pick the three highest-friction problems

Research fragmentation

Several teams independently researching the same user workflow.

Pattern fragmentation

Products solve tables, filtering, navigation, reporting and permissions differently.

Workflow fragmentation

Every designer has a different working relationship with PM and Engineering.

UX visibility

Leadership sees outputs, like Figma files, but not UX outcomes.

Likely candidates; the final three come from audit evidence. NN/g cautions against implementing the whole DesignOps menu at once.

Introducing DesignOps · Phase 2

10

Now the conversation runs on evidence rather than opinion. Don't try to fix everything: choose roughly three problems. The right DesignOps activities depend on the organization's highest-value problems. Source: NN/g DesignOps FAQ.

Cross-team critique is one of the fastest ways to break silos. Within a few months designers start discovering duplicated work, and that moment of recognition is exactly the organizational learning we want.

Phase 3 · UX guilds

Overlay guilds instead of reorganizing

UX Research Guild

3–5 designers · 1 steward

Research standards, insight tagging, study reuse.

Design System Guild

3–5 designers · 1 steward

Patterns, contributions and adoption across products.

Accessibility Guild

3–5 designers · 1 steward

Accessibility criteria built into reviews and handoff.

UX Measurement Guild

3–5 designers · 1 steward

Outcome metrics and the quarterly impact review.

Guilds sit across today's structure and distribute DesignOps, so it never depends on one Principal Designer.

Introducing DesignOps · Phase 3

16

Don't reorganize the department yet. Overlay communities of practice across the existing product teams. With twenty designers, four guilds of three to five people each, with one steward per guild, covers the main shared capabilities.

Introducing DesignOps · Phase 3

17

Design systems often stall as component libraries. Define three layers. For a mature SaaS platform, the experience-pattern layer delivers the most value: shared models for complex, cross-product workflows save teams weeks and make the customer experience coherent.

Phase 4 · Months 3–5 · Govern lightly

Three levels of decisions

1

Team decision

Designer + PM + Engineering. Most work stays here.

2

Shared-pattern decision

Design system / guild. When something affects multiple products.

3

Experience-level decision

Principal / design leadership. When cross-product behavior changes.

Centralize standards. Decentralize execution.

Introducing DesignOps · Phase 4

18

Avoid central approval for everything. Bars on the right show relative volume: most decisions stay with the team; fewer reach the guilds; only a few need design leadership. The operating principle: centralize standards, decentralize execution.

Phase 4 · Definition of UX quality

A shared bar before major releases

Appropriate research or evidence considered

Existing patterns evaluated

Design system used

Content reviewed

Not every box needs a meeting. The checklist sets expectations.

Introducing DesignOps · Phase 4

19

A Definition of UX Quality is a lightweight, shared bar for major experiences before they ship. It is a set of organizational expectations, not a series of review meetings.

Phase 5 · Months 4–6 · Impact

Report impact, not output

Stop reporting

“UX completed 37 projects.”

Run a quarterly UX Impact Review instead. Exact measures depend on the product.

Customer

  • Faster task completion
  • Fewer support tickets
  • Higher usability

Product

  • Higher adoption
  • Higher engagement
  • Higher completion

Delivery

  • Less design-to-dev rework
  • More design-system adoption
  • Less duplicated research

Organization

  • More research reuse
  • More cross-team collaboration
  • More UX visibility

Introducing DesignOps · Phase 5

20

This is the part many DesignOps programs miss. Report outcomes across four lenses: customer, product, delivery and organization. DesignOps research commonly cites standardization, designer enablement, scaling design's reach, productivity, collaboration and quality as major sources of value. Source: NN/g, DesignOps definitions.

The trap to avoid

If I personally keep every template, critique, repository, research request and design-system decision, I've created a new silo: me.

Introducing DesignOps · Execution

23

The Principal Designer should not become the permanent DesignOps manager. The job is to architect the system, distribute ownership through guilds and stewards, prove impact, and institutionalize it.

After 90 days, leadership can answer

What is UX learning? Where are we duplicating effort? Which customer problems are we solving across products? What measurable impact is UX creating?

And the final: do all of our products feel like one connected platform, rather than products designed by separate teams?
